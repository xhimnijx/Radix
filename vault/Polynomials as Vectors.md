# Polynomials as vectors

A member of `R_q` is 128 numbers. That is a point in 128-dimensional space, or a
vector in a **lattice**: the set of all integer combinations of some basis vectors.

This note builds the bridge from "128 numbers" to "a hard geometric problem".

## From coefficients to a lattice

The lattice used by Radix is generated by a single matrix `A` whose entries are
polynomials in `R_q`. Concretely, `A` is `k × ℓ` with `k = 4`, `ℓ = 13`, and each
entry is 128 coefficients, so `A` is a 512 × 1664 matrix over `Z_q`.

The **image lattice** of `A` is:

$$\mathcal{L}(A) = \{\, A s \bmod q \;:\; s \in \mathbb{Z}^{1664}\,\}$$

A public key is one point of this lattice, `pk = A·s`, where `s` is a *short* vector
of small coefficients.

## The two problems

### SIS: Short Integer Solution

> Given `A` and a target `t` in the image, find a **short** `s` with `A s = t`.

This is what a signature does: it proves knowledge of a short preimage. If SIS is
hard, then producing such an `s` for a `t` you did not choose is hard, and
extracting someone else's `s` from their signature is hard.

The "short" is essential: without a size restriction you could solve the linear
system with Gaussian elimination. The hardness lies entirely in finding a **short**
solution.

### LWE: Learning With Errors

> Given `A` and `b = A s + e` where `e` is small, recover `s`.

This is the same idea with noise added; it is what protects the commitments in the
scheme.

Both are believed hard against quantum computers. No efficient quantum algorithm is
known for either, which is why lattice cryptography is the leading post-quantum
family.

## What "short" means quantitatively

Radix measures length with the **ℓ₂ norm** and the **infinity norm**:

$$\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum v_i^2}, \qquad \|v\|_\infty = \max_i |v_i|$$

Coefficients live in `[0, q)`, but a coefficient near `q−1` means `−1`, so we always
take the **centred representative** in `(−q/2, q/2]` before measuring. This is
`ring.centered`.

Concretely:

- the secret `s` has coefficients in `[−5, 5]`: norm ≈ 25 on a 128-coefficient
  polynomial;
- the masked response `z` has coefficients around `±2¹¹`: norm ≈ `2^11.4`;
- the verifier checks `‖z‖₂ ≤ β` with `β = s·√(2Wd)` ([[Norms and Why They Matter]]).

## The hinge: why the mask must be big

If the prover sent `z = y + c·s` with a *small* `y`, then `z` would reveal `c·s` and
hence `s`. The mask `y` must be large enough that `z` is statistically independent of
`s`. Too large and the proof is huge and the verifier's bound is loose (weakening
security in a different way). The calibrated answer is in
[[Norms and Why They Matter]].

## The dimension count that matters

For the `A` relation at `k = 4`, `ℓ = 13`, `d = 128`:

- the kernel of `A` has dimension `(ℓ − k)·d = 9·128 = 1152`,
- `det(A)` has size `q^{k·d} = q^{512}`,
- the Gaussian-heuristic shortest vector in the kernel is
  `(q^{512})^{1/1152} ≈ q^{4/9} ≈ 2^14.22`.

Any `s` with norm below that is *the* short solution; the security claim is that
finding one for a random target requires more work than the target security level.
See [[Security Analysis]] for the exact numbers and
[[The Parameters and Where Every Number Comes From]] for provenance.

## Why 1664?

`ℓ · d = 13 · 128 = 1664` coordinates. This appears repeatedly as the denominator in
norm bounds and in the M-SIS targets, so it is worth remembering as **the width of
the secret**.
