Both reviewers answered the seventh pass and converged. Three things changed in the code.

TRUNCATION. C1 and C2 both caught that an 8-sigma tail of 2^-49.5 per coefficient is not
a 128-bit claim, and C2 computed the aggregate: sqrt(4224) * 2^-49.5 is about 2^-43.5,
which cannot inherit the paper's 2^-100 HVZK clause. Tables are now generated at 12 sigma:
tail 2^-107.8 per coefficient, about 2^-101.8 over a response, which is below the paper's
bound. The tables grew from ~300 KB to ~440 KB total, which is the entire cost.

RING DIGEST. The ring digest now absorbs the modulus, the ring degree, the depth and the
proved matrix A before the keys, in both languages. Without them a proof could be
replayed against a different parameter set or a different A, which is precisely the
substitution the digest exists to prevent. Both cross-language fixtures were regenerated
and each implementation still accepts the other's proof at 21,760 bytes.

The 2x slack. C2 found the thing my tests did not probe: the relaxed range proof's lemma
guarantees a bound of 2B, not B, because the slack is on the maximum. The new test records
what this implementation actually does -- its verifier bound is calibrated at the maximum
of Rf over an honest f, so a vector doubled is rejected, which is the safe direction --
and asserts the supply arithmetic the factor of two feeds into. A 64-bit amount does not
fit one coefficient of q ~ 2^32, which is why the base-4 encoding exists.

Recorded rather than implemented, because both are new protocol pieces: the stealth public
key is P = A*p + e1, NOT A*p, so it is not a SMILE key at all and needs a noise-aware
[A | I_k] statement; and the sender-computable tag needs a public T_K = A'*k in the
meta-address. C2 also showed that a uniform XOF(S) does not merely break the norm, it
breaks decryption.
